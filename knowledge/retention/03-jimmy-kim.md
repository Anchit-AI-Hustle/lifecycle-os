## 1. Who he is and why he matters

Jimmy Kim is the "infrastructure" voice of this knowledge pool. Where Chase Dimond speaks from the agency seat and Eli Weiss from the brand seat, Kim speaks from the seat of someone who *builds the sending platform itself* — which means his content is unusually strong on the mechanics everyone else hand-waves: deliverability, sender reputation, data structure, triggers, and what actually happens between "send" and "inbox."

He describes himself as an email marketer of 17+ years and a 4x founder, and his defining professional arc is the founding of **Sendlane**, an email/SMS platform purpose-built for ecommerce retention that competes directly with Klaviyo. Because Sendlane's positioning is literally "unified retention marketing platform for email, SMS, reviews, and forms," Kim's public content doubles as an ongoing education program in retention economics — he needs the market to understand retention to sell his product, and that makes him prolific.

## 4. The Kim playbook: distinctive ideas worth internalizing

- **The 20/60/20 audience model.** His signature framework: every list splits into a bottom 20% who will never purchase, a middle 60% with average LTV, and a top 20% of high-LTV VIPs — and each demands a different retention strategy. Goal: move prospects to first purchase, average buyers to repeat purchase, and VIPs into continuous cycles. He calls audience management the #1 thing most brands need to fix before anything else.
- **"Hammer vs. nurture."** A critical distinction between marketing to *prospects* (where urgency and frequency — hammering — can work) versus *customers* (where nurture, education, and relevance protect LTV). Most brands apply one mode to both audiences and burn one of them.
- **Frequency can improve deliverability.** A counterintuitive, platform-data-backed stance: sending more often to properly engaged segments improves sender reputation and engagement, whereas infrequent blasts to the whole list destroy it. The real variable is segmentation discipline, not send count.
- **Click data as the feedback loop.** Post-Apple-Mail-privacy, he pushes clicks (not opens) as the behavioral signal to build preference models from — what people click tells you what to send next.
- **Retention revenue as a planning number.** His 20–30% benchmark isn't a vanity stat; he frames it as a budgeting tool — if you're doing $100K/month, $20–30K should be arriving on autopilot from flows, abandoned-cart recovery, welcome conversion, and campaigns. If it isn't, you have a systems problem, not a creative problem.
- **Tool-stage matching.** He's candid that starter tools (e.g., Mailchimp) are fine pre-product-market-fit, and platforms like his only pay off once there's traction and data depth. This "right tool for the stage" honesty is rarer than it should be among founders selling software.

## 6. How to use him (and how not to)

Use Kim to build your mental model of the retention *machine*: how data, triggers, deliverability, and audience tiers interact; how to benchmark retention revenue share; how to think about ESP selection and migration; and how to structure segments so frequency helps rather than hurts. He's also the most useful voice here for anyone who might one day evaluate Sendlane vs. Klaviyo vs. Omnisend — he argues his book, but he explains the underlying platform mechanics better than neutral reviewers do.

Do not treat his platform comparisons as neutral (he is Klaviyo's competitor), and don't expect deep brand-side storytelling or CX texture — that's Eli Weiss and Joanne Coffey territory. Cross-check any Sendlane-favorable claim against a second source before making stack decisions.

## 8. Field checklist: installing Kim-style audience management

His platform-seat teachings, converted into an implementation sequence that works on any ESP:

1. **Build the 20/60/20 segments today.** Define VIPs (top ~20% by LTV or 3+ orders), core (1–2 orders / average LTV), and never-buyers (subscribed 90+ days, zero purchases). Every campaign brief must now name which tier it serves and what cycle-move it's attempting (prospect→first purchase, buyer→repeat, VIP→continuity).
2. **Split "hammer" and "nurture" calendars.** Promotions, urgency, and high frequency go to engaged prospects and lapsing deal-responsive segments; customers get education, replenishment, and relevance-led sends. Auditing last month's sends against this rule usually reveals you hammered your best customers.
3. **Create engagement tiers for frequency, not a global cadence.** 30/60/90-day engagement segments with escalating frequency for the most engaged and automatic suppression/sunset for the disengaged — this is how "send more" and "protect deliverability" coexist.
4. **Instrument click-preference tagging.** Every campaign link should write a profile property (category, product line, content type). Within a quarter you have declared-by-behavior preference data driving flow branches — his post-open-rate feedback loop.
5. **Stand up the retention revenue dashboard.** One number reviewed weekly: owned-channel (email+SMS) share of total revenue, decomposed into flows vs. campaigns. Target band 20–30%; flows should carry the majority. If campaigns dominate, your automation layer is underbuilt.
6. **Match the tool to the stage honestly.** Pre-traction: cheapest adequate ESP, focus on capture and welcome. Post-traction: migrate for data depth (behavioral triggers, per-product events) before adding channels. His own stated criterion — "when you need deeper data" — is the migration trigger, not a follower count.
7. **Pick up the phone for reviews and saves.** His most human tactic: for high-value customers at churn risk or post-negative-experience, a call outperforms any automation and produces the most memorable recoveries.
