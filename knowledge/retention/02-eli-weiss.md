## 1. Who he is and why he matters

Eli Weiss occupies a lane almost nobody else in this pool does: he built his reputation not by sending emails but by running the customer experience and retention functions inside two of the most-studied DTC brands of the 2020s — OLIPOP and Jones Road Beauty — and then publicly documenting what worked. In his own recounting (Martech Family interview), when he had roughly 1,000 Twitter followers in 2020 he was "the only one talking about CX and retention in a sea of growth guys," which is what made founders, investors, and operators cluster around his account.

His core thesis, repeated across every interview: brands treat CX as a cost center and retention as an email program, when in reality the two are one system. Support tickets, returns data, review sentiment, and post-purchase education are retention inputs, and the brands that win (his canonical examples: Zappos, Chewy) win primarily on experience. That framing is now mainstream in DTC; he was one of the earliest and loudest voices behind it.

## 3. Verified brand work and documented results

**At OLIPOP** — the functional soda brand that scaled from a hard sell ("a $3 can of soda in Walmart in 2019 was absurd") to a category leader — his CX team was given license to do surprise-and-delight at scale. Documented examples he has shared in interviews: sending a $50 DoorDash gift card to a customer who couldn't get soda during a hurricane; sending flowers to a family whose mother had enjoyed OLIPOP in hospice care. The strategic point he draws from these: emotional connection compounds into retention and word-of-mouth in ways that a discount cannot, and CX latitude is a growth investment, not a cost.

**At Jones Road Beauty** — he entered knowing nothing about makeup ("I never used makeup before... I had no idea about the space"), so he ran a research-first playbook: mining returns data and social channels, and interviewing 50+ consumers on video calls before scaling anything. The two initiatives he says "worked crazy well," both independently discussed by Jones Road teammates and partners:

1. **Concierge shade matching** — remote, human-assisted shade matching before purchase, which attacks the #1 return/churn driver in complexion products (wrong shade).
2. **Customer education across the journey** — teaching customers how to actually use the products (e.g., the famous WTF "What The Foundation" launch content), which he credits with increasing LTV and reducing returns.

His tenure overlaps with Jones Road's record-setting Black Friday/Cyber Monday that was up over 150% year-over-year — a result notable enough that Klaviyo cited it on a public earnings call (a rare, genuinely third-party verification of a DTC retention program's performance).

**At Yotpo** — his verified role is essentially "in-house brand operator inside a SaaS company": hiring brand-side operators, challenging the company's transactional reputation, and shaping the retention product narrative. His observation from this seat (recent LinkedIn posts): retention teams at top-50 DTC brands are now making decisions that used to belong to growth/paid teams — pop-up logic, opt-in software, landing-page offers — because retention economics now subsidize acquisition.

## 4. The Weiss playbook: distinctive ideas worth internalizing

- **Transparency as strategy.** "Put what's behind the curtain out in front." Proactive honesty during delays, stockouts, and mistakes builds more retention than polished silence. This came directly out of the FUGU delayed-shipping crucible.
- **Education is the highest-ROI retention flow.** Post-purchase education (how to use, how to combine, what to expect) lifts LTV and cuts returns — measurable at Jones Road. Before optimizing win-back discounts, fix the "customer didn't get the promised outcome" leak.
- **CX must be embedded, not adjacent.** He put CX team members inside marketing meetings, 3PL/ops meetings, and product meetings "to be the eyes and ears of the customer." Retention marketing without a feedback loop from support is flying blind.
- **Qualitative before quantitative.** The 50+ customer interviews at Jones Road preceded the dashboards. His consistent advice to brands: talk to churned customers and read tickets before buying tools.
- **Surprise-and-delight has real unit economics.** Not random gifting — targeted, story-generating gestures for high-emotion moments, which convert into UGC, reviews, and repeat purchase.

## 6. How to use him (and how not to)

Use Eli when you are designing the *system around* email/SMS: post-purchase journeys, support-to-marketing feedback loops, education flows, returns reduction, and the org design of a retention team. He is the best source in this pool for making CX measurable and commercially legible to a founder or CFO.

Do not go to him for channel mechanics — deliverability, Klaviyo segment logic, SMS compliance, subscription dunning. He openly positions himself as strategy/experience-side; for mechanics, pair him with Jimmy Kim, Joanne Coffey, or Gina Perrelli from this pool. Also note his current incentive: he works for Yotpo (reviews/loyalty/SMS platform), so platform-adjacent recommendations should be read with that in mind, though he is unusually candid about the bias.

## 8. Field checklist: installing the CX-to-retention loop

The Weiss system, converted into things a team can actually implement this quarter:

1. **Tag every support ticket by root cause,** not just topic — wrong expectation set, product misuse, shipping, quality, sizing/shade. Review the distribution monthly *in the retention meeting*, because each root cause maps to a fixable flow (expectation-setting email, education content, proactive delay notice).
2. **Interview churned and at-risk customers on video.** His Jones Road number was 50+ interviews before scaling anything; even 10 will reshape your win-back copy. Ask what they expected, what happened, and what would have changed the outcome — then quote their language back in flows.
3. **Build one education flow before your next discount flow.** Between purchase and delivery, send usage content (how to apply/use/combine); after delivery, send outcome content. Measure return rate and second-purchase rate against a pre-launch baseline — the two metrics his Jones Road education work moved.
4. **Give CX a surprise-and-delight budget with trigger rules,** not vibes: define the high-emotion moments (life events surfaced in tickets, service failures, milestone orders) and the spend cap per gesture. Track resulting reviews/UGC so the budget defends itself.
5. **Put a CX representative in marketing, ops, and product meetings** with one standing agenda item: "what are customers telling us this week?" This is his literal org design, and it costs nothing.
6. **Pre-write your transparency templates.** Delay notices, stockout notes, and mistake apologies written *before* the crisis — candid, specific, unsigned-off-by-legal-into-mush — are the FUGU lesson institutionalized.
7. **Audit the gap between marketing promise and delivered experience** quarterly: read your top three ads, then your top three one-star reviews, side by side. The delta is your churn driver list.
